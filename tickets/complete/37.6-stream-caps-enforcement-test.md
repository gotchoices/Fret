## What shipped

New spec `packages/fret/test/rpc.stream-caps.spec.ts` (168 lines). No behavior change in `src/` —
the only source edit is a corrected comment inside `classify()`.

Two in-memory nodes, one connection, one handler registered through `registerRpcHandler` at
`maxInboundStreams: 2`. The handler parks on a gate so admitted streams are held open at once; four
concurrent `rpcRequest` calls (each with a `decode`, so the sender stays parked reading) run over
that single connection. A convergence poll waits until every request has either entered the handler
or already failed — the window in which the cap is binding — then the gate opens and the admitted
requests complete. The whole case runs twice, once per transport: `createMemNode` (memory +
plaintext + yamux) and `createMemoryNode` (TCP + noise + yamux).

### The two measured facts

- **No off-by-one.** `maxInboundStreams: N` admits exactly N concurrent inbound streams per protocol
  per connection. At cap 2 with 4 concurrent requests: 2 admitted, 2 refused, on both transports.
- **A remote's inbound-cap refusal reaches the sender as `decode-error`, not `unreachable`** —
  `FrameTruncationError: stream ended before a framed message arrived`. The remote aborts the muxed
  stream, what arrives is end-of-stream rather than a reset, so `readFramed` raises truncation and
  `classify()` takes the `isFrameTruncationError` arm. `noteRpcFailure` treats `decode-error` as
  relevance decay with **no contact strike**, so a peer refusing at its inbound cap is not escalated
  toward `dead`. The harm the old residual warned about does not occur on either transport tested.

That second fact contradicted a claim written into both `src/rpc/request.ts` (the `NOTE:` in
`classify()`) and `docs/fret.md` (*Stream management*). Both were corrected to say
observed-not-guaranteed and to name the test; `REFUSED_KIND` in the spec is the single constant that
fails if a libp2p upgrade turns that teardown back into a reset.

## Review findings

**Read first:** the implement diff (`512d5ca`) end to end before the handoff summary, plus
`test/helpers/libp2p.ts`, `sendFramed`'s signature, and the open ticket board for site claims.

**Correctness / design — nothing found.** The three things most likely to make a cap test lie are
each handled and each stated at the site: the requester dials **once** so every request reuses one
connection (a second connection would silently invalidate the per-connection cap); the handler parks
on a gate so admitted streams are genuinely concurrent rather than serialized; and the sender passes
a `decode` so it stays parked reading instead of returning `ok` right after the write. The refused
arm asserts the absence of `local-limit` as well as the presence of `decode-error`, which is what
keeps the inbound (remote's ceiling) and outbound (our ceiling) arms from being conflated — the one
assertion whose omission would have made the case pass for the wrong reason.

**Resource cleanup — checked, correct.** The gate is opened in `after()` as well as in the case
body, so a case failing before the release cannot strand a parked handler for the exit watchdog.
`stopAll` runs newest-first and is best-effort per node. The spec's own `unhandledRejection` guard
is a deliberate copy of its siblings rather than a shared import, matching house precedent. One
ordering nit not worth changing: the `process.off` hook is registered before the node-teardown hook,
so a rejection thrown *during* teardown escapes the guard — the same shape every sibling rpc spec
has, and teardown here is two `node.stop()` calls.

**Hygiene — acceptable.** 168 lines, one `it`, parameterized over a two-entry transport table. The
handler ignores `sendFramed`'s boolean backpressure return, which is correct for a ~15-byte reply.
Comment density is high but every block states a decision (why two transports, why one dial, why a
gate, why `REFUSED_KIND` is a named constant) rather than restating code.

**Docs — verified against the new reality, not assumed.** Both corrected passages were read in full
against the measured outcome. `docs/fret.md`'s *Stream management* now records the no-off-by-one
result in the backstop-arithmetic paragraph and the corrected teardown shape in the residual
paragraph; the `classify()` `NOTE:` matches it word for word on the substance. No other file
mentions the residual — checked.

**Tripwire recorded (not a ticket).** The poll loop is the one timing-shaped construct in the spec.
It is a convergence wait (20 ms steps, 5 s ceiling), not a fixed sleep, and hitting the ceiling fails
loudly on the `admitted + refused === CONCURRENT` assertion rather than passing silently — but it is
where flake would appear under a loaded CI box. Parked as a `NOTE:` at the poll site in
`test/rpc.stream-caps.spec.ts` saying to raise the ceiling rather than replace the poll with a sleep
if it ever flakes.

**Two gaps the implementer named honestly — both already claimed, no new ticket filed.** A site-claim
grep over the open board found `tickets/implement/37.7-stream-caps-profile-test.md` already covering
`streamCaps()` and `maxOutboundStreams`, and already carrying the "confirm nothing assumes the two
profiles are symmetric" item. Per the architecture-first rule these are arms of that ticket, not new
instances, so an arm was appended to it rather than a fresh ticket filed:
- `maxOutboundStreams` has **no positive coverage anywhere** — this spec asserts it only negatively.
  The appended arm asks 37.7 to drive a local ceiling for real and assert `kind: 'local-limit'`,
  no contact strike, and a `diag.streamLimit` increment.
- The symmetry sweep is restated as still open, with the scope named (a read-only pass over
  `streamCaps()`'s callers and the four registrar functions).

**Single-library caveat, restated rather than dismissed.** Both facts are two transports, one libp2p
version, one muxer (yamux). Nothing here proves the behavior across muxers, and the docs and the
`classify()` note both say observed-not-guaranteed for exactly that reason.

**Major findings: none.** No new `fix/`, `plan/`, or `backlog/` tickets were created — every gap
found was either fixed inline, recorded as a tripwire, or an arm of an already-open ticket.
